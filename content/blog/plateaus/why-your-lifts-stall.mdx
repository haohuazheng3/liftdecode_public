Every lifter hits the same wall. The programme that added ten kilos to your squat in three months adds nothing in the next three. The obvious move is to do more: another set, another day, another exercise. Most of the time that is exactly the wrong move, because the thing that stopped scaling was never volume.

## A plateau is a signal, not a verdict

Progress in the gym is the output of a handful of inputs: the load and volume you train with, how well you recover between sessions, how much you eat, and how accurately you measure whether any of it is working. When you were new, every input had slack in it. Any reasonable programme pulled on all of them at once and the bar went up.

That slack runs out unevenly. One input becomes the limiting factor while the others still have room. Adding more training when the limiting factor is sleep does nothing except dig the hole deeper.

## The four places a stall actually lives

**Progression that outran recovery.** Linear progression works until the weekly jump exceeds what your body can adapt to in a week. The tell is missed reps on the last set for two or three sessions in a row, with everything else unchanged. The fix is a smaller jump, not a bigger deload.

**Volume that is too low for where you are now.** Three hard sets per muscle per week is plenty for a beginner and nowhere near enough two years in. If you are recovering easily, sleeping well and eating enough, and the lifts are still flat, this is the one to check.

**Food that stopped matching the work.** A surplus of two hundred calories disappears the moment your training volume goes up. If you have not gained any weight in six weeks and you are trying to add muscle, the programme is not the problem.

**Measurement that cannot see slow progress.** Two kilos on a lift over eight weeks is real progress. It also looks like nothing on a week-to-week basis. Lifters who only track top sets routinely call a working plateau a dead one.

## Change one thing, then wait

The instinct when stalled is to overhaul everything. That guarantees you will never know what worked. Pick the most likely limiter, change only that, and give it three to four weeks before you judge it. Keep a log of working weights, reps in reserve and bodyweight, because the answer is usually already in the numbers you were not writing down.

<Callout title="Quick check">
If you cannot say, from your log, what your best set of five was six weeks ago, start there. You cannot diagnose a stall you cannot see.
</Callout>

The good news is that stalls are rarely mysterious. They are just specific, and the fix is specific too.
